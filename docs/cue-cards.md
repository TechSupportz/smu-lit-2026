Reusable Claimant's Cue-Card Template
DATE / STAGE	WHAT HAPPENED?	WHAT I SHOULD SAY	WHY IT MATTERS
[DATE] — BEFORE THE TRANSACTION	What did I originally want or expect? What did I see, hear or understand before entering the transaction?	“I went there because I wanted/needed ______. I understood that ______.”	Establishes the Claimant's original intention and expectations.
[DATE] — INITIAL REPRESENTATION	What did the Respondent first tell me? What price, service, product or promise was represented?	“I was told ______. I understood this to mean ______.”	Identifies the representation or promise made by the Respondent.
[DATE] — DURING THE TRANSACTION	What happened when the transaction was being made? Was there pressure, confusion, a change in price, additional charges, or other conduct?	“During the transaction, ______ happened. I felt/understood ______.”	Connects the Respondent's conduct to the legal issue.
[DATE] — AGREEMENT / PAYMENT	What did I eventually agree to or pay? Was it different from what I originally expected?	“I eventually paid/agreed to ______, but this was different from what I originally understood because ______.”	Anticipates the Respondent's argument that “you agreed to it.”
[DATE] — AFTER THE TRANSACTION	What happened when I left? When did I realise there was a problem?	“After I left, I realised ______.”	Shows when the Claimant became aware of the problem.
[DATE] — FIRST COMPLAINT	How quickly did I complain? What did I ask the Respondent to do?	“I contacted/returned to the Respondent on ______ and asked for ______.”	Shows promptness and consistency of the complaint.
[DATE] — RESPONDENT'S RESPONSE	What did the Respondent say or do in response?	“The Respondent refused/said ______.”	Establishes the dispute between the parties.
[DATE] — STEPS TAKEN	Did I contact CASE, make a police report, send correspondence, or otherwise try to resolve the matter?	“I then tried to resolve the matter by ______.”	Shows that the Claimant attempted to resolve the dispute rather than immediately commencing proceedings.
HEARING — RESPONDENT'S ARGUMENT	What is the Respondent likely to say?	“The Respondent may say ______. My response is ______.”	Directly addresses the other side's case.
HEARING — LEGAL CONNECTION	What legal rule applies to these facts?	“I respectfully submit that these facts show ______ under [relevant law].”	Converts the facts into a legal argument.
CONCLUSION	What do I want the Tribunal to do?	“For these reasons, I respectfully ask the Tribunal to ______.”	Clearly states the remedy sought.
The 4-Step Method for Every Event

For each important event, use this simple formula:

1. WHAT HAPPENED?

State the fact.

“I went to the shop for a haircut.”

2. WHAT DID I UNDERSTAND?

Explain your understanding or expectation.

“I understood that I was paying $20 for a basic haircut.”

3. WHAT DID THE RESPONDENT DO?

Identify the Respondent's conduct.

“After the service, I was told that I had to pay $100 for an upgraded package.”

4. WHY DOES THIS MATTER?

Connect it to your case.

“This is important because I did not go there intending to purchase the $100 package.”

This keeps the Claimant from simply telling a story. Every important fact has a purpose.

The "5 Questions" Template

For an elderly self-represented Claimant, I would also put these five questions at the top of the cue card:

1. WHAT DID I WANT?

“I went there to ______.”

2. WHAT WAS I TOLD?

“I was told ______.”

3. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED?

“Instead, ______ happened.”

4. WHAT DID I DO WHEN I REALISED THERE WAS A PROBLEM?

“I immediately ______.”

5. WHAT DO I WANT THE TRIBUNAL TO DO?

“I respectfully ask the Tribunal to ______.”

If the Claimant can answer those five questions clearly, she can usually explain the core of her case without having to memorise complicated legal submissions.

How to Turn Facts Into a Legal Argument

The important distinction is:

Don't say only:

“The Respondent treated me unfairly.”

Instead, build the argument:

Fact → Problem → Law → Result

For example:

STEP	TEMPLATE
FACT	“I went to the Respondent to ______.”
WHAT CHANGED	“I was initially told ______, but later ______.”
WHY IT WAS A PROBLEM	“I did not understand/expect/agree to ______.”
MY CIRCUMSTANCES	“At that time, I was ______.”
WHAT I DID	“I complained/asked for a refund on ______.”
LEGAL POINT	“I respectfully submit that these circumstances show ______ under ______.”
REMEDY	“I therefore ask for ______.”
Handling the Respondent's Defence

This is particularly important in SCT cases.

Don't wait for the Respondent to make an argument before thinking about it. Prepare a “They say / I say” card.

THE RESPONDENT MAY SAY…	MY RESPONSE…
“You agreed to it.”	“I understand that I eventually agreed/paid. But I ask the Tribunal to consider the circumstances in which I came to agree.”
“You signed the document.”	“I understand that I signed it. However, I ask the Tribunal to consider whether I understood what I was signing and the circumstances in which I signed it.”
“You received the service/product.”	“I understand that I received it. My complaint is about ______.”
“You changed your mind.”	“I did not simply change my mind. I complained ______ [hours/days] later because ______.”
“You could have said no.”	“At the time, I felt ______ and understood ______. I ask the Tribunal to consider the circumstances I was in.”
“You should have read the terms.”	“I understand that I should take care with documents. However, my case is that ______.”
“There was no pressure.”	“My evidence is that I felt ______ because ______. I ask the Tribunal to consider the circumstances as a whole.”

Important: Only use a response if it is actually supported by the Claimant's evidence. Do not invent facts simply because they would make the argument stronger.

A Good Chronological Structure

For the final written submission, I would generally arrange it like this:

1. BEFORE

What did I want?

↓

2. DURING

What was I told?

↓

3. THE PROBLEM

What changed or went wrong?

↓

4. MY CIRCUMSTANCES

Why was I particularly affected by what happened?

↓

5. AFTER

What did I do when I realised there was a problem?

↓

6. RESPONDENT

What did the Respondent say or do?

↓

7. LAW

Why do these facts satisfy the legal requirements?

↓

8. REMEDY

What am I asking the Tribunal to order?

Golden Rules for Preparing the Cue Cards
Rule 1 — Keep each card to one point

Don't put a whole page on one cue card.

Instead:

23 OCTOBER — I WENT FOR A HAIRCUT
“I only wanted a simple haircut. I did not intend to buy an expensive package.”

Then:

23 OCTOBER — WHAT I WAS TOLD
“I was told the haircut would cost $20.”

This makes it much easier to follow during a hearing.

Rule 2 — Use ordinary language first

Don't make the elderly Claimant say:

“The Respondent exerted undue influence within the meaning of s 4(d).”

Instead:

“I felt pressured and afraid, and I did not feel that I was making a free decision.”

Then the written submission can explain how those facts relate to s 4(d).

Rule 3 — Don't exaggerate

Say:

“I felt pressured.”

Not:

“They deliberately manipulated me.”

unless there is evidence supporting that statement.

Credibility is extremely important.

Rule 4 — Don't argue every fact

Ask:

“Does this fact help prove my case?”

If it doesn't, leave it out unless it is necessary to explain the story.

Rule 5 — Always explain the significance

After every important fact, ask:

“Why does this matter?”

That is what turns a story into a legal case.

Rule 6 — Finish with a clear request

Never leave the Tribunal wondering what you want.

End with:

“For these reasons, I respectfully ask the Tribunal to order the Respondent to ______.”

WHAT I DID AFTERWARDS
“When I realised there was a problem, I ______ on ______.”

RESPONDENT'S ARGUMENT
“The Respondent may say ______.”

“My response is ______.”

LEGAL POINT
“I respectfully submit that these facts show ______ under [statute/legal principle].”

“I respectfully ask the Tribunal to ______.”

The overarching principle is: tell the story in dates, identify the problem, explain why it matters legally, answer the Respondent's argument, and finish by asking for a specific remedy.
